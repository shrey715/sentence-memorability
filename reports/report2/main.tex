\section{Introduction and Background}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Overview}

Human memory for language is not a faithful archive of words and
syntax. A central finding in cognitive psychology is that listeners
and readers retain the \emph{meaning} of sentences with much greater
fidelity than their verbatim wording or grammatical structure.
This dissociation raises a set of tightly linked empirical questions:
What specific semantic properties of words drive sentence encoding?
Does the grammatical form of a sentence leave an independent trace in
memory? And when surface form is partially forgotten, on what basis do
speakers reconstruct it?

The present study addresses these questions by embedding three
classic research programmes into a single large-scale
online experiment. We examine how the \emph{imageability} (also termed
\emph{memorability} or \emph{imagery value}) of a sentence's constituent
nouns affects (a)~recognition of sentence meaning, and (b)~retention of
grammatical voice, using a continuous recognition paradigm administered
via the internet.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Background and Theoretical Motivation}

Sentence memory can be broadly divided into \textbf{verbatim memory}
(the retention of exact wording and syntactic structure) and
\textbf{gist memory} (the retention of overall meaning). Extensive
research demonstrates that gist memory typically persists much longer
than verbatim memory, as people tend to rebuild sentences based on
meaning rather than recalling the exact syntactic form.

The earliest and most influential formal account of this phenomenon,
the \emph{kernel-plus-tag} hypothesis, was proposed by Miller (1962)
and Mehler (1963). This model suggested that sentences are reduced to
a stripped-down ``kernel'' proposition, with syntactic features (such as
passive voice or negation) stored as independent, forgettable memory
trace components. \citet{bregman1968} tested and discredited this
model. They found that when participants misidentified the syntactic
form of a sentence on a first guess, their second guesses were reliably
above chance ($\chi^2(4) > 20$, $p < .001$), indicating that
participants were not retrieving an all-or-none syntactic tag. Instead,
syntactic form is often reconstructed from other memories, such as
imagery and noun salience.

\citet{begg1971} examined this dissociation directly using a
\textbf{continuous recognition paradigm}, in which participants encounter
a continuous, uninterrupted stream of sentences and make recognition
judgments. Begg found that the accuracy of meaning judgments decreased
with lag ($r = -.996$), while wording judgments were uncorrelated with
lag ($r = -.107$). Following Begg and Paivio (1969), who hypothesised
that the meaning of a concrete sentence is represented as a spatial image
while wording is a verbal string, Begg proposed that exact wording is
reconstructed from the image trace at retrieval rather than stored
independently.

If wording is reconstructed from an image trace, the properties of
the nouns composing that image are critical.
\citet{james1973} tested this in a free-recall paradigm using active
and passive sentences. They systematically varied the imagery value of
subject and object nouns to create four sentence types: HH, HL, LH, and
LL. They documented a bias whereby speakers place the most salient aspect
of a situation in the subject position, as well as a bias favouring
active sentences over passive ones during reconstruction.

Together, this framework suggests that sentence memory is fundamentally
semantic and imagistic: wording is reconstructed rather than retrieved,
and reconstruction is anchored by the imageability of constituent nouns.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Research Hypotheses}

The present experiment draws upon the continuous recognition paradigm and
noun-imageability manipulations to test the following hypotheses:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{H\arabic*:}]
    \item \textbf{(Gist/Meaning):} Sentence memorability, measured via
    Corrected Immediate Recognition (IR), differs across noun imageability
    conditions (HH, HL, LH, LL).
    \item \textbf{(Verbatim/Wording):} Wording Recognition (WR) accuracy
    is independent of the sentence's semantic trace and will not be
    significantly affected by noun imageability conditions.
    \item \textbf{(Voice Effect):} Based on reconstructive biases
    documented by \citet{james1973}, active-voice sentences will show
    different memorability and wording-recognition patterns compared to
    passive-voice sentences.
    \item \textbf{(No Interaction):} Grammatical voice does not moderate
    the noun imageability effect on either Corrected IR or WR accuracy.
\end{enumerate}

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Present Study in Context}

This experiment extends \citet{james1973} from free recall to a
continuous recognition paradigm, tests Begg's prediction that
imageability selectively affects gist but not wording retention, and
does so at online scale with $N = 112$ participants. The dual IR+WR
response design enables a within-paradigm dissociation of semantic and
syntactic memory traces.

Report~2 additionally covers analyses not included in Report~1:
Generalized Linear Models (GLM) with heteroscedasticity-robust inference,
Signal Detection Theory (SDT) decomposition of sensitivity ($d'$, $A'$)
and criterion ($c$), and a block-level learning analysis examining whether
the imageability effect changes across the three experimental blocks.

\section{Methods}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Participants}

Data were collected from online participants via a web-based continuous
recognition experiment. A total of 114 participants started the
experiment. Two participants failed the vigilance validation criterion on
all three blocks and were excluded entirely, leaving \textbf{112
participants} in the final sample. The block pass rate was
\textbf{96.2\%} (329 out of 342 total blocks).

\subsection{Design}

The study used a fully within-participants
\textbf{4 (Noun Condition: HH, HL, LH, LL) $\times$ 2 (Voice: Active,
Passive)} design. Noun conditions were defined by crossing the imageability
level of the subject and object nouns in each sentence
(Table~\ref{tab:conditions}).

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{lll}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Code} & \textbf{Subject Noun} & \textbf{Object Noun} \\
    \midrule
    HH & High imageability & High imageability \\
    HL & High imageability & Low imageability \\
    LH & Low imageability & High imageability \\
    LL & Low imageability & Low imageability \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \caption{The four experimental noun conditions, defined by crossing
  subject and object noun imageability.}
  \label{tab:conditions}
\end{table}

Grammatical voice was fully crossed with noun condition, yielding
\textbf{8 within-participant cells}. An additional set of
high-frequency filler sentences (coded HF) served as recognition lures
for computing false alarm rates.

\subsection{Stimuli}

Simple Subject--Verb--Object (SVO) sentences were constructed by
systematically varying the imageability of subject and object nouns.
High- and low-imageability nouns were selected from established word
memorability norms; verbs were drawn from a fixed set of
medium-concreteness action verbs to minimise between-sentence variability.

Sentences were kept short ($\leq$8 words), contained no proper nouns,
and used natural spoken English. Automated fluency and semantic-coherence
filters removed ungrammatical or implausible items, followed by manual
screening. From the resulting pool, 200 active-voice sentences were
selected (50 per condition). Each was converted into a paired passive-voice
form, yielding \textbf{400 target sentences} across 8 conditions.
An additional set of HF filler sentences provided false alarm
opportunities (lures to which a correct response is \emph{not}
pressing IR).

\subsection{Procedure}

Participants completed a \textbf{continuous recognition} task. Each
participant saw 48 target sentences across \textbf{3 blocks}. Each
sentence was displayed for approximately 4000~ms, followed by an
inter-trial interval of 700--1300~ms. Probes appeared for target
sentences, and participants made two sequential judgments:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Immediate Recognition (IR):} ``Have you seen this sentence
  before?'' (spacebar press = yes).
  \item \textbf{Wording Recognition (WR):} ``Does the grammatical voice
  match the sentence you originally saw?'' (key press = yes/no).
  WR was only recorded when IR was pressed.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Preprocessing and Data Pipeline}

The raw behavioural data, comprising 81,329 event-log rows from 114
participants, underwent a multi-stage preprocessing pipeline in R.

\subsubsection{Data Cleaning}

Individual participant \texttt{.log} files were combined into a single
long-format dataset. The raw logs are one-row-per-event: each stimulus
display (\texttt{Sentence shown}) and each response
(\texttt{IR pressed}, \texttt{WR pressed}) are separate rows. Relevant
columns include \texttt{participant\_id}, \texttt{event\_type},
\texttt{noun\_condition}, \texttt{voice}, \texttt{is\_target\_sentence},
\texttt{is\_probe\_repeat}, \texttt{ir\_accuracy},
\texttt{wr\_accuracy}, and reaction times.

Cleaning steps:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Filtering:} Removed practice trials,
  inter-stimulus-interval rows (\texttt{gap\_time}), and rest-phase
  markers.
  \item \textbf{Trial scoring:} Binary accuracy computed from button-press
  values on \texttt{IR pressed} and \texttt{WR pressed} rows.
  \item \textbf{Feature extraction:} Noun condition and voice parsed
  from stimulus IDs.
  \item \textbf{Outlier policy:} No reaction time outlier exclusions;
  non-parametric tests are used throughout, which are robust to extremes.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Vigilance Validation}

Each block was retained only if:
\[
  \text{Correct Validation IRs} >
  \frac{\text{Wrong Validation IRs}}{2} + \text{Missed Validation IRs}
\]

Failed blocks were excluded. This yielded a final dataset of
\textbf{48,281 observations from 112 participants}. The design
remained perfectly balanced: each of the four noun conditions
contributed exactly 1,316 encoding trials, and each voice condition
contributed exactly 2,632 trials (658 trials per experimental cell).

\subsubsection{False Alarm Rate Computation}

A key methodological note: \texttt{ir\_accuracy} is \texttt{NA} on all
\texttt{Sentence shown} (display) rows in the event log --- accuracy
values are only populated on \texttt{IR pressed} (response) rows. False
alarm counts were therefore computed by filtering to
\texttt{event\_type == "IR pressed"} on HF first-showing trials
(i.e., presses that constituted false alarms). The denominator
was the number of HF first-showing display events per participant.
This yields:
\[
  \widehat{\text{FA rate}} = \frac{\text{IR presses on HF first-showings}}
                                  {\text{HF first-showing display events}}
  \quad (M = 0.160, \; SD = 0.104, \; \text{range: } 0.00\text{--}0.43)
\]
A prior version of the code incorrectly filtered on
\texttt{Sentence shown} rows, which always yielded
$\widehat{\text{FA}} = 0$, causing Corrected IR to equal the raw hit
rate. This has been corrected for all analyses in this report.

\subsection{Dependent Variables}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Corrected IR:} Per participant per condition,
  $\text{IR}_{CR} = \text{Hit Rate} - \text{FA Rate}$. This isolates
  true recognition discriminability from response bias.
  \item \textbf{WR Accuracy:} Proportion of correct voice judgments,
  conditional on IR being pressed.
  \item \textbf{IR Reaction Time (RT):} Mean RT in milliseconds from
  target probe trials.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Statistical Analysis Plan}

\subsubsection{Normality Assessment}

Shapiro-Wilk tests were run per condition group (4 conditions
$\times$ 3 metrics = 12 tests). All IR~CR and WR~Accuracy cells were
significantly non-normal ($p < .001$), justifying non-parametric
inference. IR~RT was also non-normal in 3 of 4 conditions.
Consequently, \textbf{Friedman's test} was used as the omnibus test
throughout (replacing Kruskal-Wallis, which assumes independent groups;
all conditions are within-participants here).

\subsubsection{Family-Wise Error Rate Control}

Three omnibus Friedman tests were run on the same dataset (IR~CR,
WR~Accuracy, IR~RT). A Bonferroni family-wise alpha was applied:
$\alpha_{\text{FW}} = 0.05 / 3 = .0167$. Within each significant omnibus
test, post-hoc paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (same participants
across conditions) were Holm-corrected.

\subsubsection{Interaction Tests}

The noun condition $\times$ voice interaction was evaluated using
\textbf{Scheirer-Ray-Hare (SRH) tests}. Cell balance was verified
before running SRH (all cells $N = 112$). SRH was chosen as the
non-parametric analogue of two-way ANOVA; a caveat is that it was
originally designed for independent groups and is used here as an
approximation in a within-participants design.

\subsubsection{Extended Analyses}

Beyond the core non-parametric tests, Report~2 includes:
(a)~\textbf{GLM} with Gaussian identity link, backward AIC selection,
and HC3 heteroscedasticity-robust standard errors;
(b)~\textbf{Signal Detection Theory (SDT)} decomposing sensitivity
($d'$, $A'$) and criterion ($c$);
(c)~\textbf{Block Learning} analysis examining condition effects across
the three experimental blocks.

\label{sec:h1}

Per-participant Corrected IR scores (collapsed across voice) by noun
condition are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ir_cr}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{plots/01_ir_cr_by_condition.png}

A \textbf{Friedman's test} (within-participants omnibus, replacing

\[
  \chi^2(3) = 22.39, \quad p < .001,

= 1.000 or $p > .40$), indicating a \textbf{threshold pattern}: the
presence of a single high-imageability noun is sufficient to anchor
sentence gist memory, and adding a second high-imageability noun
provides no further benefit.

  \label{fig:interactions}
\end{figure}

Scheirer-Ray-Hare tests (2-way non-parametric; Figure~\ref{fig:interactions})
found for \textbf{Corrected IR}: a main effect of noun condition
($H = 11.26$, $p = .010$, $\eta^2_H = 0.013$); no main effect of voice
($H = 0.76$, $p = .384$, $\eta^2_H = 0.001$); and \textbf{no
interaction} ($H = 0.22$, $p = .975$, $\eta^2_H = 0.000$).
For \textbf{WR accuracy}: no significant main effects or interaction
(all $p > .23$). These results support H4 (no interaction).

\textbf{Note on test statistic discrepancy:} The SRH noun condition
effect ($H = 11.26$, $p = .010$) is smaller than the marginal Friedman
statistic from Section~\ref{sec:h1} ($\chi^2(3) = 22.39$, $p < .001$)
because SRH partitions the total sum of squares across the full
$4 \times 2$ factorial structure, whereas the Friedman test operates on
the marginal distribution collapsed across voice. The two tests are
not contradictory --- they address different questions (marginal condition
effect vs.\ condition effect in the presence of voice).

Sensitivity checks: Friedman's tests run separately within each voice
level confirmed the noun condition effect for Active sentences
($\chi^2(3) = 11.09$, $p = .011$) but not for Passive alone
($\chi^2(3) = 7.68$, $p = .053$). These sensitivity checks are treated
as \textbf{exploratory secondary analyses} and are not subject to the
family-wise $\alpha_{\text{FW}} = .0167$ correction applied to the three
primary omnibus tests. The Active $p = .011$ falls below the uncorrected
.05 threshold but should not be interpreted as confirmatory.

\section{Signal Detection Theory Analysis}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Rationale}

Corrected IR (hit rate $-$ FA rate) conflates sensitivity and response
criterion. Signal Detection Theory (SDT) decomposes performance into
\textbf{sensitivity} ($d'$: how well a participant distinguishes targets
from lures) and \textbf{criterion} ($c$: the decision threshold, whether
a participant is liberal or conservative). This allows us to test whether
the LL deficit reflects reduced discriminability, a criterion shift, or
both. $A'$ (a non-parametric area-under-the-ROC proxy) provides a
robustness check that does not assume equal variance.

\textbf{Caveat:} All HF lures are active-voice. A global FA rate is
therefore used for both voice conditions, which may artificially inflate
$d'$ for passive sentences. SDT voice comparisons should be interpreted
with this limitation in mind.

The LL condition shows the lowest $d'$ (1.925), highest $c$ (0.107, a
conservative shift), and lowest $A'$ (0.876). HH and HL are identical
in $d'$ (2.146), consistent with the threshold pattern in Corrected IR.

\subsection{Inferential Tests on $d'$ and $A'$}

Shapiro-Wilk tests on $d'$ per condition group confirmed normality
($p > .09$ in all four groups), justifying parametric repeated-measures
ANOVA for $d'$ and $c$. For $A'$ (bounded [0.5, 1]), Friedman's test
was used.

\textbf{Note on test selection:} The normality assumption failed for
Corrected IR and WR Accuracy at the participant level (all cells
$p < .001$, Section~2.6.1), necessitating non-parametric inference in
Section~3. However, $d'$ values are z-score transformations derived from
larger trial pools and are structurally more Gaussian; per-group
Shapiro-Wilk tests confirmed normality in all four conditions
($p > .09$), justifying the switch to RM-ANOVA in this section.

\textbf{RM-ANOVA on $d'$}: Mauchly's sphericity test was
non-significant ($W = 0.961$, $p = .492$), so sphericity was assumed.
\[
  F(3, 333) = 8.47, \quad p < .001, \quad
  \text{GES} = 0.018, \quad \text{Cohen's } f = 0.134 \; (\text{small})
\]
Post-hoc paired $t$-tests (Holm-corrected) confirmed that LL was
significantly lower than HH ($p < .001$, $d = 0.39$), HL ($p < .001$,
$d = 0.43$), and LH ($p = .010$, $d = 0.29$). HH vs.\ HL
($p = .996$) and HH vs.\ LH ($p = .768$) were non-significant,
replicating the threshold pattern.

\textbf{Friedman's test on $A'$}:
\[
  \chi^2(3) = 23.50, \quad p < .001,
  \quad \text{Kendall's } W = 0.070 \; (\text{small})
\]
Post-hoc paired Wilcoxon (Holm-corrected): LL lower than HH
($p = .002$, $r = .360$), HL ($p < .001$, $r = .415$), and LH
($p = .031$, $r = .256$). HH--HL and HH--LH non-significant
($p > .50$).

\subsection{Criterion ($c$) Analysis}

RM-ANOVA on $c$ produced the same $F$-statistic as $d'$ (they share the
same within-participant rank structure given the global FA rate):
$F(3, 333) = 8.47$, $p < .001$. The LL condition shows the most
positive $c$ ($M = 0.107$), indicating a \textbf{conservative shift}:
participants required stronger evidence to call a sentence ``seen'' when
it contained two low-imageability nouns. This is a new insight beyond
Corrected IR alone --- the LL deficit is not purely a sensitivity
reduction but also involves a criterion adjustment.

\section{Generalized Linear Model Analysis}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Rationale and Model Selection}

GLMs were fit to examine multivariate predictors of Corrected IR and
WR Accuracy at the cell level (one row per participant $\times$ noun
condition $\times$ voice; $N = 890$ rows after listwise exclusion of
6 participant-by-cell rows with zero WR trials following block-level
failures, reducing from 896). The reference levels were LL (noun condition) and
Active (voice). Models were selected via backward AIC stepwise
elimination from a full main-effects and interaction model. Because
Breusch-Pagan tests indicated heteroscedasticity in both outcomes
(IR: $p = .040$; WR: $p = .002$), \textbf{HC3 heteroscedasticity-robust
standard errors} were used for all inference. No Cook's D values
exceeded~1, confirming the absence of high-influence observations.

\section{Block Learning Analysis}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Rationale}

Participants saw 48 target sentences across 3 blocks. The block
learning analysis tests whether recognition accuracy improves across
blocks (a pure practice/exposure effect) and whether the noun condition
effect is stable across blocks or diminishes as participants learn
(a condition $\times$ block interaction).

\subsection{Block Main Effect}

Friedman's tests collapsed across noun condition confirmed a significant
block effect on Corrected IR:
\[
  \chi^2(2) = 23.30, \quad p < .001,
  \quad \text{Kendall's } W = 0.111 \; (\text{small})
\]
Post-hoc paired Wilcoxon (Holm-corrected): Block 1 was significantly
lower than Block 2 ($p < .001$, $r = .351$) and Block 3
($p < .001$, $r = .449$). Block 2 vs.\ Block 3 was non-significant
($p = .166$, $r = .142$), indicating most learning occurs between
Blocks 1 and 2. No significant block effect on WR Accuracy
($\chi^2(2) = 1.42$, $p = .492$) or RT ($\chi^2(2) = 0.51$,
$p = .773$).

A Scheirer-Ray-Hare test on the full block dataset found:

\textbf{noun condition $\times$ block interaction $H = 6.43$, $p = .377$} ---
non-significant. The LL deficit is present and stable across all three
blocks; it does not attenuate with exposure.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Summary of Findings}

This study embedded the classic sentence memorability paradigms of
\citet{begg1971} and \citet{james1973} into a large-scale online
continuous recognition task, testing how noun imageability influences
semantic recognition (Corrected IR) and syntactic retention (WR
Accuracy) across 112 participants.

The results support a \textbf{threshold model of semantic imageability}.
Corrected IR was significantly impaired only when \emph{both} the
subject and object nouns were of low imageability (the LL condition;
Friedman's $\chi^2(3) = 22.39$, $p < .001$). The presence of even a
single high-imageability noun --- HH, HL, or LH --- produced uniformly
high recognition performance, and these three conditions were
statistically indistinguishable from one another. This threshold
pattern replicated in $d'$ (RM-ANOVA $F(3,333) = 8.47$, $p < .001$)
and $A'$ (Friedman's $\chi^2(3) = 23.50$, $p < .001$) from the SDT
analysis, and was stable across all three experimental blocks.

The SDT criterion analysis adds a new finding: LL sentences also induced
a \textbf{conservative criterion shift} ($c_{\text{LL}} = 0.107$ vs.\
$c_{\text{HH/HL}} = -0.003$). Participants required greater subjective
evidence before pressing IR on LL sentences. This suggests the LL
deficit is not purely a perceptual/sensitivity reduction but also
involves a metacognitive adjustment in response strategy.

Crucially, while noun imageability affected the retention of
\emph{meaning}, it had \textbf{no effect on wording recognition}.
WR accuracy was uniformly high and significantly above chance across all
conditions (both voices, $r > .83$ vs.\ chance). Furthermore, there was
no main effect of grammatical voice on either metric, no noun condition
$\times$ voice interaction (Friedman within each voice; SRH; GLM Wald
and Bayes; all consistent), and GLM Bayesian comparison yielded very
strong evidence for the null on WR (BF$_{01} = 1027$).

A positive trial position effect on Corrected IR emerged in the GLM
($\hat{\beta} = 0.011$ per unit position, $p < .001$), consistent with
the block learning analysis showing IR improves from Block 1 to Block 2.
This reflects growing familiarity with the task and stimuli, not
condition-specific learning, since no block $\times$ condition
interaction was found.

\subsection{Theoretical Implications}

These findings align strongly with the dual-process framework proposed
by \citet{begg1971}. The dissociation --- noun imageability drives
semantic recognition but leaves wording recognition unaffected ---
supports the notion that meaning is stored holistically (as an image
trace) while surface wording is reconstructed separately at retrieval.

The absence of a voice effect on either IR or WR provides further
evidence against the kernel-plus-tag hypothesis
\citep{bregman1968}. If grammatical voice were stored as an
independent, fragile tag appended to a semantic kernel, passive
sentences would recognise differently from active ones and would
interact with noun saliency. Instead, participants' robust
above-chance ability to detect voice mismatches across all conditions
suggests that syntactic form is \emph{reconstructed} at retrieval,
anchored by semantic imagery rather than stored as an autonomous tag.

The threshold pattern specifically addresses \citet{james1973}'s free-recall
findings. James et al.\ observed graded recall benefits from
high-imageability nouns; the present continuous recognition paradigm
reveals a step function: any high-imageability anchor is sufficient.
This may reflect the difference between free recall (where every
retrieval cue matters additively) and recognition (where a single
vivid anchor suffices to trigger a familiarity signal above the
decision threshold).

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Sample size:} $N = 112$ is below the pre-registered power
  analysis target of 334. Underpowered for detecting small effects,
  particularly on WR and in block-level interaction tests.
  \item \textbf{One-sided lures:} All HF lures are active-voice. The
  global FA rate therefore conflates active-voice lure responses with
  passive-voice target contexts, potentially artificially elevating
  $d'$ for passive sentences in the SDT analysis.
  \item \textbf{SRH approximation:} The Scheirer-Ray-Hare test was
  originally designed for independent groups. Its use here (within-participants
  design) is an approximation and may be conservative.
  \item \textbf{Online administration:} Lack of control over viewing
  conditions, distractions, and screen size introduces noise absent in
  laboratory settings.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future Directions}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Reach target $N$:} Recruiting to $N = 334$ would provide
  adequate power for small-effect WR and voice analyses.
  \item \textbf{Voice-matched lures:} Including passive-voice HF lures
  would allow separate FA rates per voice, enabling unbiased SDT voice
  comparisons.
  \item \textbf{EEG correlates:} Examining N400 or late positive
  component amplitudes during encoding would ground the imageability
  effect in neural correlates of semantic processing.
  \item \textbf{Naturalistic stimuli:} Testing with multi-clause
  naturalistic sentences would assess whether the threshold model
  generalises beyond controlled SVO materials.
  \item \textbf{Individual differences:} Exploring whether working
  memory capacity or vocabulary breadth moderates the LL deficit would
  illuminate the cognitive mechanism underlying the threshold pattern.
\end{itemize}
