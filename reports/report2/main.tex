\section{Introduction and Background}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Overview}

Human memory for language is not a faithful archive of words and syntax.
A central finding in cognitive psychology is that listeners and readers
retain the \emph{meaning} of sentences with much greater fidelity than
their verbatim wording or grammatical structure. The present study
addresses three tightly linked questions: What properties of nouns drive
sentence encoding? Does grammatical form leave an independent trace? And
when surface form is partially forgotten, on what basis is it reconstructed?

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Background and Theoretical Motivation}

Sentence memory can be divided into \textbf{verbatim memory} (exact
wording and syntax) and \textbf{gist memory} (meaning). The
\emph{kernel-plus-tag} hypothesis (Miller, 1962; Mehler, 1963) proposed
that sentences are reduced to a semantic kernel with syntactic features
stored as forgettable tags. \citet{bregman1968} refuted this: when
participants misidentified the syntactic form on a first guess, their
second guesses exceeded chance ($\chi^2(4) = 20.8$, $p < .001$),
indicating that grammatical form is reconstructed from imagery and noun
salience rather than retrieved as a tag.

\citet{begg1971} tested this dissociation directly via a \textbf{continuous
recognition paradigm}. Meaning judgments declined sharply with lag
($r = -.996$), while wording judgments were uncorrelated with lag
($r = -.107$). Following Begg and Paivio (1969), this was interpreted as
evidence that concrete sentence meaning is stored as a spatial image, while
wording is reconstructed from the image trace at retrieval.

If wording is reconstructed from an image, the imageability of constituent
nouns is critical. \citet{james1973} tested this in free recall using four
conditions: HH, HL, LH, LL. They documented a threshold-like recall pattern
and a bias toward placing salient nouns in subject position during
reconstruction, with passive-to-active transformation errors more frequent
when the object noun was highly imageable (LH). Together, these studies
establish that sentence memory is fundamentally semantic and imagistic.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Signal Detection Theory: Literature Background}
\label{sec:sdt_lit}

A limitation of raw accuracy metrics (hit rate, corrected IR) is that
they confound \emph{sensitivity} --- the fidelity of the memory trace ---
with \emph{response criterion} --- the decision threshold a participant
adopts. Signal Detection Theory (SDT), developed formally by
\citet{green1966}, provides a principled decomposition.
Under SDT, a recognition memory task is modelled as a decision between two
overlapping Gaussian distributions: the noise distribution (lures) and the
signal distribution (targets). A participant's memory report is determined
jointly by trace strength and a freely-chosen criterion $c$.

The sensitivity index $d'$ measures the standardised distance between
signal and noise distributions: $d' = z(\text{HR}) - z(\text{FAR})$.
The criterion $c = -0.5[z(\text{HR}) + z(\text{FAR})]$ captures liberal
or conservative response bias. When $c > 0$, a participant requires
stronger evidence before claiming recognition. Applied to the imageability
manipulation, SDT allows us to distinguish whether LL sentences are poorly
recognised because participants \emph{cannot} discriminate them from lures
(reduced $d'$) or because they \emph{choose} a higher evidence threshold
when uncertain (elevated $c$), or both.

\citet{macmillan2005} established the methodological standards for SDT
in recognition memory, including the log-linear correction for extreme
hit and false alarm rates, and the non-parametric $A'$ index for
robustness to the equal-variance Gaussian assumption. \citet{snodgrass1988}
introduced the $A'$ formula applied here:
\[
  A' = 0.5 + \frac{(\text{HR} - \text{FAR})(1 + \text{HR} - \text{FAR})}
                   {4 \cdot \text{HR} \cdot (1 - \text{FAR})}
  \quad (\text{when HR} \geq \text{FAR})
\]
$A'$ is bounded in $[0.5, 1]$, requires no distributional assumptions, and
serves as an independent check on $d'$ conclusions. Prior applications of
SDT to sentence memory have been limited; this study is among the first to
decompose imageability-driven recognition effects into sensitivity and bias
components explicitly.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Research Hypotheses}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{H\arabic*:}]
  \item \textbf{(Gist):} Corrected IR differs across noun imageability
  conditions (HH, HL, LH, LL).
  \item \textbf{(Wording):} WR accuracy is not significantly affected by
  noun imageability; participants can reconstruct voice from the imagistic trace.
  \item \textbf{(Voice):} Active-voice sentences will show different
  memorability and wording-recognition patterns compared to passive.
  \item \textbf{(No Interaction):} Voice does not moderate the noun
  imageability effect on either Corrected IR or WR.
\end{enumerate}

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Present Study in Context}

This experiment extends \citet{james1973} from free recall to continuous
recognition, tests \citet{begg1971}'s prediction that imageability selectively
affects gist but not wording, and applies SDT to decompose the imageability
effect into sensitivity and criterion components. Four analytic frameworks
are used in parallel: non-parametric Friedman tests (primary); GLM with
robust inference; SDT; and block-level learning analysis.

\section{Methods}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Participants}

Data were collected from 114 online participants. Two participants failed
the vigilance criterion on all three blocks and were excluded, leaving
\textbf{112 participants} (block pass rate: 96.2\%, 329/342 blocks).

\subsection{Design}

Fully within-participants \textbf{4 (Noun Condition: HH, HL, LH, LL)
$\times$ 2 (Voice: Active, Passive)} design. Noun conditions were defined
by crossing subject and object noun imageability (Table~\ref{tab:conditions}).
High-Frequency (HF) filler sentences served as lures for false alarm
estimation.

    \bottomrule

  \caption{Four experimental noun conditions.}
  \label{tab:conditions}

\subsection{Stimuli}

Simple SVO sentences were constructed by crossing noun imageability
levels with voice. High- and low-imageability nouns were drawn from
established word memorability norms; verbs were medium-concreteness
action verbs. After fluency and coherence filters, 200 active-voice
sentences (50 per condition) were selected and each converted to passive,
yielding \textbf{400 target sentences} plus HF lure sentences.

\subsection{Procedure}

Participants completed a continuous recognition task across
\textbf{3 experimental blocks} (preceded by a practice block). Each
sentence was displayed for $\approx$4000~ms (ITI: 700--1300~ms). For
each probe, participants made two sequential judgments:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{IR:} ``Have you seen this sentence before?'' (spacebar).
  \item \textbf{WR:} ``Same grammatical voice?'' (key press; elicited
  only when IR was pressed).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Preprocessing and Data Pipeline}

Raw event logs from 114 participants (81,329 rows) were processed in R.
Events were cleaned, block boundaries identified from ``Rest Phase
started'' markers, and per-trial accuracy variables extracted from
\texttt{IR pressed} and \texttt{WR pressed} rows.

\subsubsection{Vigilance Validation}

Each block was retained only if:
\[
  \text{Correct Validation IRs} >
  \tfrac{\text{Wrong Validation IRs}}{2} + \text{Missed Validation IRs}
\]
Failed blocks were excluded; two participants failing all blocks were
removed entirely, leaving 48,281 observations. The design was balanced:
each noun condition contributed 1,316 encoding trials; each voice
condition 2,632 trials.

\subsubsection{Practice Block Exclusion}

The practice block was excluded from all analyses, including the
block-level learning analysis. Practice trials serve a single purpose:
familiarising participants with the interface --- learning which keys to
press, understanding the response sequence, and adapting to the timing
of the display. The sentences themselves are not drawn from the
experimental stimulus set and do not carry over into experimental blocks.
Including practice data would introduce noise from the rate at which
individual participants adapt to a novel test environment, a process
that is irrelevant to the study's question of how noun imageability
affects sentence memory. The block-level analysis tests whether the LL
deficit changes across successive \emph{experimental} exposures
(Blocks 1, 2, 3); this question is only answerable cleanly if every
observation reflects fully-familiarised participants responding to proper
experimental stimuli.

\subsubsection{False Alarm Rate Computation}

False alarm counts were computed by filtering to \texttt{IR pressed}
events on HF first-showing trials; the denominator was the number of
HF first-showing display events per participant.
\[
  \widehat{\text{FA rate}} = \frac{\text{IR presses on HF first-showings}}
                                  {\text{HF first-showing display events}}
  \quad (M = 0.160,\; SD = 0.104,\; \text{range: } 0.00\text{--}0.43)
\]

\subsection{Dependent Variables}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Corrected IR:} $\text{IR}_{CR} = \text{Hit Rate} - \text{FA Rate}$.
  \item \textbf{WR Accuracy:} Proportion of correct voice judgments,
  conditional on IR pressed.
  \item \textbf{IR Reaction Time (RT):} Mean RT on correct target probes.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Statistical Analysis Plan}

\subsubsection{Normality Assessment and Test Selection}

Shapiro-Wilk tests per condition group confirmed that Corrected IR and
WR Accuracy are non-normal in all four groups ($p < .001$), expected
given that these are bounded proportions computed from 3--6 trials per
cell. Non-normality justifies \textbf{Friedman's test} as the omnibus
statistic. Friedman's is the within-participants equivalent of
Kruskal-Wallis: it blocks on participant identity (removing
between-person variance), ranks observations within each participant,
and tests for systematic rank differences across conditions --- all
without distributional assumptions.

For SDT indices, $d'$ and $c$ are theoretically unbounded z-score
transforms and passed normality per group ($p > .09$), justifying
\textbf{repeated-measures ANOVA} for those specific indices. $A'$ is
bounded in [0.5, 1] and uses Friedman's by design.

\subsubsection{Family-Wise Error Rate Control}

Three omnibus Friedman tests on the same dataset (IR CR, WR Accuracy,
IR RT) inflate the family-wise error rate; a Bonferroni correction was
applied: $\alpha_{\text{FW}} = 0.05/3 = .0167$. Within significant
omnibus tests, Holm-corrected paired Wilcoxon signed-rank post-hoc
tests were conducted. Holm's sequential procedure is uniformly more
powerful than Bonferroni while controlling FWER.

\subsubsection{Interaction Tests}

Noun condition $\times$ voice interactions were evaluated with
\textbf{Scheirer-Ray-Hare (SRH) tests} --- the non-parametric two-way
analogue of ANOVA. Cell balance was confirmed ($N = 112$ per cell).
SRH was designed for independent groups; its use here (within-participants)
is an approximation; sensitivity Friedman tests per voice level follow
as a robustness check.

\subsubsection{Extended Analyses}

(a)~\textbf{GLM}: Gaussian identity link, backward AIC selection from a
full main-effects and interaction model, HC3 heteroscedasticity-robust
standard errors (sandwich estimator), Bayesian model comparison via
\texttt{lmBF()};
(b)~\textbf{SDT}: RM-ANOVA on $d'$ and $c$ (normality confirmed),
Friedman's on $A'$, convergent validity via Spearman $\rho(d',
\text{Corrected IR})$;
(c)~\textbf{Block Learning}: Friedman block main effect, per-block
noun condition Friedman, SRH condition~$\times$~block interaction.

\section{Signal Detection Theory Analysis}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Rationale}

As established in Section~\ref{sec:sdt_lit}, Corrected IR conflates
sensitivity and response criterion \citep{green1966,macmillan2005}.
SDT allows us to test whether the LL deficit reflects reduced
discriminability ($d'$ lower), a conservative criterion shift ($c$ higher),
or both. This distinction has direct theoretical implications: a pure
criterion shift would suggest that LL sentences generate \emph{some}
memory trace but participants distrust it, whereas a sensitivity
reduction indicates a genuinely weaker imaginal representation.

SDT parameters were computed per participant per noun condition using
log-linear correction (adding 0.5 to counts, 1 to denominators) to
prevent infinite z-values \citep{macmillan2005}.

\textbf{Caveat:} All HF lures are active-voice; a global FA rate
is therefore used for both voice conditions, which may marginally inflate
$d'$ for passive sentences. Voice SDT comparisons are interpreted with
this limitation in mind.

\subsection{Inferential Tests on $d'$ and $A'$}

Shapiro-Wilk per group confirmed normality of $d'$ ($p > .09$ all groups),
justifying RM-ANOVA. $A'$ is bounded and uses Friedman's.

\textbf{RM-ANOVA on $d'$}: Mauchly's $W = 0.961$, $p = .492$ (sphericity
assumed).
\[
  F(3, 333) = 8.47,\quad p < .001,\quad
  \text{GES} = 0.018,\quad \text{Cohen's } f = 0.134 \;(\text{small})
\]
Holm-corrected post-hoc paired $t$-tests: LL < HH ($p < .001$, $d = 0.39$),
HL ($p < .001$, $d = 0.43$), LH ($p = .010$, $d = 0.29$); HH vs.\ HL
($p = .996$) and HH vs.\ LH ($p = .768$) non-significant. Threshold
pattern confirmed.

\textbf{Friedman's on $A'$}: $\chi^2(3) = 23.50$, $p < .001$,
$W = 0.070$. Post-hoc Holm: LL < HH ($r = .360$), HL ($r = .415$),
LH ($r = .256$); HH--HL, HH--LH non-significant ($p > .50$).
The $A'$ result, which makes no equal-variance assumption
\citep{snodgrass1988}, replicates the $d'$ pattern, confirming it is
not an artefact of the SDT model's distributional assumptions.

\subsection{Criterion ($c$) Analysis}

RM-ANOVA on $c$: $F(3, 333) = 8.47$, $p < .001$. The LL condition
shows the most positive $c$ ($M = 0.107$ vs.\
$c_{\text{HH/HL}} = -0.003$): participants required \emph{more}
subjective evidence before calling an LL sentence ``seen.''
This conservative shift occurs above and beyond the sensitivity
reduction ($d'$ is also lowest for LL), suggesting the LL deficit
comprises both a weaker imaginal trace \emph{and} a compensatory
metacognitive adjustment in decision threshold.

\section{Generalized Linear Model Analysis}

\subsection{Rationale and Model Selection}

GLMs were fit at cell level (one row per participant $\times$ noun
condition $\times$ voice; $N = 890$ after listwise exclusion of 6 rows
with no WR trials). Reference levels: noun condition = LL, voice =
Active. Backward AIC stepwise elimination from a full model including
main effects and the noun condition $\times$ voice interaction.
Breusch-Pagan tests indicated heteroscedasticity for both outcomes
(IR: $p = .040$; WR: $p = .002$); \textbf{HC3 robust standard errors}
were applied throughout. No Cook's D exceeded 1.

\section{Block Learning Analysis}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Rationale and Practice Block Exclusion}

The block learning analysis asks whether gist recognition changes across
the three experimental blocks and whether the LL deficit is stable or
attenuates with repeated exposure. The practice block was excluded for
two reasons. First, its sentences are not from the experimental stimulus
set and do not re-appear in subsequent blocks, so their retention is
irrelevant to the study's questions. Second, and more importantly,
practice block performance reflects the rate at which individual
participants adapt to a novel test environment --- learning how to press
the right buttons, calibrating to the display pace, and building
confidence in the response paradigm. This adaptation rate is a property
of the participant's interaction with the interface, not of noun
imageability processing. Including it would contaminate the Block 1
baseline with individual-level task-familiarisation noise, obscuring any
genuine learning effects driven by cumulative semantic exposure.

\subsection{Block Main Effect}

Friedman's test (collapsed across noun condition) on Corrected IR:
\[
  \chi^2(2) = 23.30,\quad p < .001,\quad W = 0.111
\]
Post-hoc Holm Wilcoxon: Block 1 < Block 2 ($p < .001$, $r = .351$)
and Block 3 ($p < .001$, $r = .449$). Block 2 vs.\ 3 non-significant
($p = .166$, $r = .142$) --- learning is front-loaded and plateaus
after the first experimental block. No block effect on WR Accuracy
($\chi^2(2) = 1.42$, $p = .492$) or RT ($\chi^2(2) = 0.51$, $p = .773$).

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Summary of Findings}

This study embedded the classic paradigms of \citet{begg1971} and
\citet{james1973} into a large-scale online continuous recognition task.

The \textbf{threshold model} of noun imageability is confirmed across all
four analytic frameworks. Corrected IR was impaired only when both subject
and object nouns were low-imageability (LL; Friedman's $\chi^2(3) = 22.39$,
$p < .001$). HH, HL, and LH were statistically indistinguishable (all
Holm-$p \geq .446$). This replicated in $d'$ (RM-ANOVA $F(3,333) = 8.47$,
$p < .001$) and $A'$ (Friedman's $\chi^2(3) = 23.50$, $p < .001$), was
stable across all three blocks (SRH interaction $p = .377$), and appeared
in near-equal GLM coefficients ($\hat\beta \approx 0.05$ for all three
non-LL conditions; BF$_{10} = 10.76$).

The SDT analysis adds a criterion component: LL sentences induce a
\textbf{conservative response shift} ($c_{\text{LL}} = 0.107$) above
and beyond reduced sensitivity. Participants appear aware of their
weaker memory and require stronger subjective evidence before reporting
recognition --- a metacognitive adjustment invisible to Corrected IR alone.

Noun imageability had \textbf{no effect on WR accuracy}. WR was robustly
above chance across all conditions (GLM: BF$_{01} = 1027$ for noun
condition null; Friedman's $p = .343$). Voice also had no effect on
either outcome, and no condition $\times$ voice interaction was detected
by any test.

\subsection{Theoretical Implications}

The meaning-wording dissociation replicates \citet{begg1971}'s central
finding in an online paradigm. Meaning recognition is driven by the
availability of an imagistic trace, anchored by at least one
high-imageability noun; wording recognition is independent of trace
strength, consistent with Begg's proposal that exact wording is
reconstructed at retrieval rather than stored. The absence of a voice
effect contradicts the kernel-plus-tag hypothesis of \citet{bregman1968}:
active and passive sentences are equally recognised and equally
discriminated, supporting the reconstruction account.

The threshold in recognition (vs.\ the gradient in \citet{james1973}'s
free recall) likely reflects a task difference: free recall requires
generating the sentence from memory (where every cue helps additively),
while recognition requires only that a trace reaches decision threshold
(where a single vivid anchor suffices).

\subsection{Limitations and Future Directions}

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item \textbf{Sample size:} $N = 112$ is below the pre-registered
  target of 334; the study is underpowered for small WR and voice effects.
  \item \textbf{One-sided lures:} All HF lures are active-voice; passive
  $d'$ may be marginally inflated.
  \item \textbf{SRH approximation:} SRH was designed for independent
  groups; its use in a within-participants design is an approximation.
  \item \textbf{Future:} Reach target $N$; include passive-voice lures;
  apply Beta regression or trial-level logistic mixed-effects models;
  explore EEG correlates (N400) of the imageability effect.
\end{itemize}
